A visual identity for Pabul, a small Korean restaurant in Lisbon’s Baixa-Chiado. We researched the place, interviewed its founder and built a brand system, with a brand book, packaging, a new facade and a digital presence.

The brief

The project started with a walk. In groups, we left the Faculty and looked for study cases within a 3 km radius: how organisations present themselves in the territory, the stories of the place and of those who made it, and the gap between the identity they want and the identity people perceive.

We chose Pabul, a small Korean restaurant on Rua do Crucifixo, in Baixa-Chiado. The task was to research it, interview its owner, write a briefing and develop a visual identity system and a brand book for it.

The system had to answer the real needs of the restaurant, be grounded in a strategic moodboard, be flexible enough to grow, and work across several contexts of application. It is hypothetical, an idealised answer to the brief we wrote.

Research · first record

About Pabul

Pabul is a Korean restaurant at Rua do Crucifixo 30, about 18 months old, run and decorated by its founder. These photographs, taken before the interview, are our first record of the place and the starting point of the research.

Rua do Crucifixo is one of the densest and most touristic streets in Lisbon, so being seen from the street is decisive for a small independent restaurant.

From outside, Pabul is a narrow entrance with a dark green frame, a round blue sign with “PABUL” in fat red letters, and a cat sticker on the door. The most active piece of communication is the chalkboard on the pavement, with the menu of the day, hand drawn illustrations and the dishes in English, Portuguese and Korean.

Inside, a long narrow room painted bright blue with red details and objects on the walls. Nothing links the facade, the interior, the menu and the digital channels: a strong personality that grew piece by piece, with character but without a conscious visual discourse.

Research · first record

The existing brand

The identity was created by James himself. The logo puts the red and blue taegeuk, from the South Korean flag, inside a bowl, with “PABUL” and “Korean Cuisine” in a classic serif. The menu is a printed sheet with his photo, social icons, a QR code and a layered Korean roof motif.

Beyond the logo and the menu, the rest is made by hand.

Research

Online, Pabul has a 9.4/10 on TheFork and 4.7/5 on TripAdvisor, but only a few reviews, and an Instagram account without regular activity. Competitors like K-BOB or Han Table Barbecue have websites, large followings and a consistent identity.

Our hypothesis was that Pabul is not a space without identity, but a space with too much of it: chaotic, accumulated, personal and not systematised. What it is does not travel outside its own walls.

The interview

We interviewed James, the founder, in the restaurant. A Korean entrepreneur and former television producer in the USA and Canada, he opened Pabul about 18 months before, to show real, unadapted Korean food in Europe. “Even if I lose money, I’m not going to lose for the Korean culture.”

He cooks, he designed the logo, the posters and the social media, and almost everything inside. “Everything is communication. Color. Posters. Signs. I create stories.” The walls of the bathroom, drawn in chalk, are a small lesson in Korean culture, from hanbok to the squid game.

The name

Pabul comes from Pa-bulgogi: “pa” is green onion, and “bulgogi” a beef dish on the menu. James shortened it because the full name was too long. When people ask what Pabul means, he offers a free drink to anyone who guesses, a small test to see who knows Korea.

The audience

About 80% of the customers are tourists who find the restaurant on Google, and the rest are locals. People understand it is Korean when they see the menu, not the facade. That meant a single identity for two audiences: tourists looking for an authentic experience, and locals who still need an accessible door into Korean culture.

The final brand

The aim was not to replace James’s voice, but to make it more visible, coherent and up to his ambition: to make this small restaurant the starting point of a Korea Town in the heart of Lisbon.

Positioning. Between Korean tradition and the city of Lisbon, Pabul stands as a place where food is a cultural act: authentic, open and with a story of its own.

Values

Authenticity. Bringing Korean culture to Lisbon without filtering it or adapting it to please, presenting it as it is.

Openness. A place where anyone can sit at a Korean table, even without knowing it.

Personality. Confident, warm, contemporary, rooted.

Identity

The story of James, from Korea through the USA and Canada to Lisbon, is the heart of the brand. We use a fusion of elements from Korean culture: the traditional clothes, the chopsticks and the typical bowl of the region.

The brand mark

The mark can be used as the symbol alone or as the logotype, depending on the context. The symbol works on its own once the brand is already recognised. The logotype is used when typographic legibility comes first, or when there is no room for the full composition.

Basic elements

The chopsticks (jeotgarak), taken from the brand mark, work as an independent graphic element, generating patterns and textures that support the visual system.

James, the mascot

James is the face of Pabul. His illustration works as the mascot of the brand, with a tone of humour and confidence that reflects the personality of the founder.

Movement

Movement is the only animated element of the system, and it belongs only to the illustration of James. Each animated version is a different expression, with its own context of use, to bring personality and humour to the brand. They are used only in digital media.

Sound

The sound of Pabul completes the visual and culinary experience. Music sets the atmosphere and reinforces the cultural authenticity of the brand: everything, from the food to the music, is authentically Korean.

Applications

The menu brings the system together: the chopstick pattern frames each section, the logo sits at the top, and James appears in the scale of spicy, from mild to super spicy. On social media, the feed alternates photography of the food and the people with typographic posts, and short animated stories tell the story of James and the restaurant. The merchandise takes James and the logotype to the back and the chest of the t-shirts.

Take away

The take away box carries the illustrated map of Lisbon, with the Pabul logotype on the side and a pair of chopsticks held by a coloured band. It comes in two versions: cream with the map in red and blue, and red with the map in white.
