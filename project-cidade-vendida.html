Lisbon’s industrial spaces, read across generations. An editorial project for Communication Design V, developed from the brief The Whole Earth Catalog Redux. The edition, Cidade Vendida, looks at how Lisbon’s industrial spaces, built for work and production, were reused to answer the needs of the contemporary city, and at how differently each generation reads them.

The brief

The main project of the semester asked us to represent particular and problematic perspectives of contemporary reality, starting from the Whole Earth Catalog, and to explore the designer as author and editor: someone who coordinates the research, the selection and the presentation of content.

Each group adapted one thematic section of the catalog to give access to situated knowledge for a specific community, framed by Donna Haraway’s idea of situated knowledges.

The work was collective and included a research dossier, a publication, an audiovisual piece and a website.

The theme

Cidade Vendida explores how spaces dedicated to work and production were transformed over the last decades. Factories, warehouses and workshops became cultural centres, creative districts and leisure destinations, shaping a Lisbon whose reading changes drastically from one generation to the next.

Reuse has become an established urban practice, and with it the way society relates to space has shifted. Projects of requalification spread across areas such as Alcântara, Marvila and Parque das Nações show how a reused space can become a device for reading the city, one that holds memory and future together, absorbing identities and reconfiguring them.

We think of these places as “soft infrastructures”, where architecture, culture and community meet and where tensions between preservation and consumption, belonging and exclusion, identity and transformation become visible.

Two cities

The edition puts two cities in dialogue: the one we inherited, and the one previous generations knew, where these same spaces had very different functions and meanings. The places we now see as icons of creativity were, for our parents and grandparents, settings of routine, effort and industry.

We walk through Lisbon with our generation’s eyes and build a visual and reflective reading of it, not as neutral observers but as inhabitants affected by its transformations. The project doesn’t look for final answers. It tries to bring out the questions that appear when a city changes faster than the generations can follow, and when a reused space stops being just a place and becomes a way of thinking about Lisbon.

The publication

The edition opens with an editorial and is built around a simple device: black-and-white archive photographs of Lisbon’s industrial spaces face colour photographs of the same places today. A fold-out collage opens the book, and every spread puts then and now side by side, so the reader does the comparing.

I researched and designed the LX Factory, Time Out Market and Parque das Nações chapters, and bound the book. The images of the future were generated with Adobe Firefly, and the publication was laid out in InDesign.
