The path of wayfinding systems in the UK. This was the first project of Communication Design III, about the path from modernism to postmodernism. In groups of two or three, we had to choose a country and research how communication design developed there from the middle of the 20th century, picking one or two designers as main references.

The brief

The theme had to come from the relationship between designers and other figures, backed by research, not from the designers’ names. From it we wrote an editorial text and designed a periodical of at least 16 pages, printed in black and white plus one spot colour. The work went through four steps: research, editing, design and a presentation to the class.

Our group was given three contexts to choose from: the United Kingdom, France and the United States. We chose the United Kingdom, a context that runs from the post-war years to post-punk, between European tradition and pop culture, and the rise of independent design.

Looking for a theme in the relationship between designers, we found wayfinding: how the need to move through increasingly complex cities pushed design to become a discipline. Two references became the core of the work: Margaret Calvert and Jock Kinneir, who redesigned the UK road signage system, and Tim Fendley and Applied, who developed Legible London.

The content was organised in Notion and the structure in Figma. We also used an interview with Margaret Calvert, in which she remembers how Jock Kinneir invited her to be his assistant, and how the new road signs started in 1957 with the motorways.

My sections

I researched and laid out the first and the last sections: A, Wayfinding in the context of (post)modernism, and D, From Point A to Point B.

The theme

A to B: The Path of Wayfinding Systems explores how wayfinding emerged to meet urban needs, prioritising clarity and accessibility. Over time, postmodernism turned it into an essential, user-centred infrastructure that reshaped the urban experience in global cities.

I focused on the legacy of Kinneir and Calvert, whose road signage set standards for clarity and cohesion, and on how Legible London turned city wayfinding into an integrated “soft infrastructure” of maps, landmarks and neighbourhood identities.

The publication

The issue opens with an editorial and is organised in four sections. The structure was decided together as a group. I researched the first and the last section and designed the publication in Adobe InDesign.

Wayfinding in Modernism and Postmodernism

Before design existed as a formal discipline, urban challenges like London’s Underground map already called for clear wayfinding. Those early solutions slowly paved the way for design as a recognised field.

Kinneir and Calvert: UK Road Signage System

How Margaret Calvert and Jock Kinneir set a standard for legibility, consistency and user-centred design, with intuitive symbols and readable typefaces, and the legacy their work left.

Tim Fendley and Applied: Legible London

A pedestrian wayfinding system that brings together signage, digital maps and public transport data, built on user research about pedestrian flow and map readability.

From Point A to Point B

A look ahead: adaptive, user-centred systems for ever-changing cities and digital integration.

The annex

Most of the images in the book are printed in the spot colour. The annex shows them with their original colours.
