O Príncipe da Neblina

2025
Role
Book design (concept, typography and layout) and bookbinding
Team
Teresa Soares
Course
Edition Design
Skills
Editorial, Layout, Bookbinding
Tools
Adobe InDesign

A redesign of Carlos Ruiz Zafón’s novel, where the fog slowly takes over the pages. Made for the Edition Design course, O Príncipe da Neblina (The Prince of Mist) grows its fog through the book until the pages turn from white to black. The stories from the past are set in navy blue, in a handwritten typeface, as if they were letters, and the dialogue is split into one column per character. I also bound the book myself.

The brief

“Com um livro faço um livro” (“With a book, I make a book”) asked each student to redesign a book of their choice, ideally one that had only been published as a conventional, text-only edition. The goal was to apply the ideas of the “hybrid novel” and “visual writing”, adding graphic elements that translate or expand the meaning of the original text.

Cover, format, materials, binding and titles (what Gérard Genette calls the paratext) had to work together as an extension of the content.

I thought of Carlos Ruiz Zafón straight away, because I knew that with any of his books I could turn a book made of words into a visual experience. The book couldn’t be too long, so I chose the last one I had read, O Príncipe da Neblina.

My first idea was to make the mystery more visible and almost turn the reader into the main character, with the clues and other things mentioned in the book becoming extra elements. I set that aside to focus on the heart of the book. So I went back to the story and realised it was really about the Prince of Mist himself, and he became my main element.

The cover

The cover is where the Prince of Mist shows up first, before the reader has opened anything. I gave it a tear, almost as if the tear were the Prince himself: something that breaks through the cover and lets the past show through.

That is how the cover brings the past and the present into the same object. The surface is the present, a closed, dark book. The tear opens it and shows there is something else underneath, which is exactly what happens in the story, where the stories from the past are what allow the kids to understand what is happening now.

The cover also sets the tone for what comes inside: the fog that grows page by page, and the past that keeps breaking into the present.

The fog

I made the book darker, in colour and in mood. I wanted to give the reader some visual tension and make the presence of the Prince of Mist more visible, so the fog increases throughout the book and the pages go from white to completely black.

It doesn’t happen all at once. The first pages are almost white, with a few grey stains drifting in from the margins. Turn the page, and the stains spread and darken until they stop being a background and become the page itself.

The type follows the fog. On the light pages the text is black. Once the paper goes dark it flips to white, so the words stay the one thing you can still make out while everything else disappears.

You can see it from the outside too. With the book closed, the edge of the pages already tells the story: pale paper at the start, darker paper at the end, a gradient you can read from the side before opening anything. By the last chapters there is almost nothing left but white type on black, as if the reader had walked into the fog as well.

The past

The book is full of flashbacks and stories from the past, and I wanted to give them importance, because those stories are what allow the kids to work out what is happening. To make the difference clear, I set them in a typeface that imitates handwriting, as if they were letters someone had written, and in navy blue. That way the reader can tell that these are the sailor’s stories and also that they belong to the past.

The columns

The book has a lot of dialogue, so I split the characters into columns, one for each of them. I wanted the reader to see the scene happening in front of them and to feel the emotions of the book more. This was the most important decision, and the one that gave the book its most visual look.

The binding

I bound the book myself, sewing the whole thing, and made the hardcover out of cardboard.

Because the whole book is full of anomalies, I carried that into the binding as well. The page numbers sit on the inside of the page, and the spine is on the inside instead of the outside.
